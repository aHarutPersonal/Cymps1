# Track Profit and Cash Separately

## Why This Matters
A business can look profitable on paper and still run out of money — this is the single most common way small businesses fail, and it is entirely avoidable once you understand the difference between profit and cash. Profit is an opinion about value earned during a period, calculated under accrual rules: revenue counts when it is earned, expenses count when they are incurred. Cash is a fact about the bank account: money either arrived or it did not. The two numbers diverge constantly. When you send an invoice, you have earned revenue (profit goes up) but no money has arrived (cash stays flat). When a customer pays a deposit for work you have not done, cash goes up but you have earned nothing yet. If you only watch one ledger, you will either overstate your health (profitable but broke, unable to pay rent) or understate it (flush with deposits but losing money on every job). This lesson builds the two-ledgers mental model that professional bookkeepers use, then drills it on a fully fictional case, Lena's Landscaping, where six everyday transactions pull profit and cash in different directions. You will classify each transaction, compute an accrual profit and a net cash change side by side, self-check against an in-app answer key, and then apply the same model cold to a new transfer case, Devon's Delivery Co. By the end, you will be able to read a small transaction list and answer the two questions every owner must answer separately: did we earn anything, and can we pay the bills?

## Core Framework
The two-ledgers mental model starts with one commitment: every transaction gets recorded twice, once on the Profit Ledger and once on the Cash Ledger, and the two records are independent. The Profit Ledger answers "what did we earn this period?" under accrual accounting. The Cash Ledger answers "what actually moved through the bank?" Neither is more correct; they measure different things.

Component 1: Revenue recognition. Under accrual rules, revenue counts when it is earned — when you deliver the goods or complete the service — not when you get paid. If Lena mows a client's lawn on June 3 and bills $800 due in thirty days, June's Profit Ledger shows $800 of revenue even though the bank balance is unchanged. The unpaid amount sits in accounts receivable, a promise from the customer. When the $900 arrives later (assume a second, smaller invoice here as our case specifies), the Cash Ledger records cash in, but the Profit Ledger records nothing new — the earning already happened. This is the first big asymmetry: earning and collecting are separate events recorded in separate periods.

Component 2: Expense recognition (the matching idea). Expenses count when the benefit is used up, not when paid. Rent for the month is an expense in that month whether paid by cash or on account. Prepayments flip the timing the other way: if Lena pays $300 upfront for six months of insurance, the cash leaves immediately, but the expense is recognized gradually as the coverage is used. In a short case, we treat the entire $300 as a cash out now while only one month's worth, $50, becomes expense — the remaining $250 sits in prepaid insurance, an asset. Timing differences like this are the engine of profit-versus-cash divergence.

Component 3: Deposits and unearned revenue. When a customer pays $500 in advance for future work, cash rises immediately but the Profit Ledger records nothing, because nothing has been earned yet. The $500 is a liability called unearned (deferred) revenue: you owe either the work or a refund. As you perform the work, you convert it to revenue. Deposits make a business look cash-rich while its profit ledger is silent — the classic overstatement trap.

Component 4: Direct cash items. Some transactions are cash-only in effect: buying $150 of supplies for immediate use is both a cash out and, once consumed, an expense. If the supplies sit in a drawer unused, they are an asset, not yet an expense. In our case we assume they are consumed in the period, so they hit both ledgers at once.

The mechanics of the summary. Build two columns. Profit column: list revenues (positive) and expenses (negative), sum to accrual profit. Cash column: list every bank receipt (positive) and payment (negative), sum to net cash change. A transaction can hit both columns, one column, or — with prepayments and deposits — hit cash now and profit later. Six transactions can therefore produce a profit figure and a cash figure that barely resemble each other, and both are legitimate.

Trade-offs. Accrual profit is the better measure of business performance — it matches effort to reward and lets you compare months fairly — but it says nothing about solvency. Cash flow is the better measure of survival — it tells you whether you can make payroll — but it is a noisy measure of performance, inflated by deposits and distorted by prepayments. Managers need both, which is exactly why standard financial reporting includes an income statement (profit) and a statement of cash flows side by side.

Two commonly confused concepts. First, receivables are not revenue "again" when collected: the collection is a cash event only. Beginners often double-count a paid invoice, inflating both ledgers. Second, money in is not the same as revenue: a loan, an owner contribution, or a customer deposit all increase cash without any earning. The Cash Ledger is blind to the difference; only the Profit Ledger sorts earning from mere receipt. Keep asking two separate questions — "was value earned?" and "did money move?" — and the model becomes mechanical.

## Worked Example
Practical scenario: Lena's Landscaping, a one-person lawn-care business, starts the month with a clean slate. Walk through all six transactions with explicit reasoning before you attempt the practice phases.

Transaction 1: Lena completes a $800 mowing-and-cleanup job for a client and sends an invoice, payable next month. Reasoning: the service was delivered, so value was earned — the Profit Ledger records $800 of revenue. No money entered the bank, so the Cash Ledger records nothing. The $800 becomes accounts receivable.

Transaction 2: Lena pays $600 cash rent for the month's storage yard. The benefit (use of the yard) is consumed in the current month, so it is an expense now: Profit Ledger shows $600 expense. Money left the bank: Cash Ledger shows $600 cash out. Both ledgers move, in opposite directions of effect.

Transaction 3: Lena pays $300 cash for six months of insurance coverage, starting now. Cash out of $300 immediately. But only one month of coverage is used this period, so the expense is one-sixth: $300 / 6 = $50. The other $250 is prepaid insurance, an asset carrying future benefit. Profit Ledger records only a $50 expense — a deliberate timing difference.

Transaction 4: A new customer pays Lena a $500 deposit for a garden redesign scheduled for next month. Cash in of $500 today. But Lena has done no work yet, so no revenue is earned; the $500 sits as unearned revenue, a liability. Profit Ledger: nothing. Cash Ledger: +$500. This transaction alone shows how cash can look healthy while earnings are zero.

Transaction 5: Lena buys $150 of fuel and mulch, paying cash, and uses them on jobs this month. Both ledgers move together: a $150 expense (the supplies are consumed) and a $150 cash out.

Transaction 6: The client from Transaction 1 pays $900 — the $800 invoice plus a $100 catch-up payment on an old job from before this case began. Cash in: $900. Profit side: the $800 was already recorded as revenue in Transaction 1, so collecting it adds no revenue. The extra $100 relates to work done before this period, so within this case we treat it as a pure collection of a prior receivable: cash in, no current revenue. (If the $100 had been for work performed this period, it would be revenue — the classification always depends on when the work was done.)

Now compute the summary. Profit column: revenue $800 (T1); expenses $600 rent (T2) + $50 insurance (T3) + $150 supplies (T5) = $800 of expenses. Accrual profit = $800 − $800 = $0. Lena broke even on earnings this month. Cash column: outflows $600 + $300 + $150 = $1,050; inflows $500 + $900 = $1,400. Net cash change = +$350. The bank balance rose by $350 in a month where the business earned nothing — entirely because of the deposit and the insurance prepayment's timing. Profit says "treading water"; cash says "money in the bank." Both statements are true, and neither alone tells the whole story. That tension is the entire point of running two ledgers.

## Failure Modes
Failure mode 1: Double-counting collected invoices. Symptom: after the client pays the $900, the learner adds $900 to revenue as well as cash, producing a phantom $800 profit. Correction: ask "when was the work done?" If the revenue was already recorded when the work was performed, the collection touches only the Cash Ledger. A paid invoice is a transfer between two assets — receivable becomes cash — not new earnings.

Failure mode 2: Treating every inflow as revenue. Symptom: the $500 deposit gets counted as revenue, inflating accrual profit to $500. Correction: apply the earning test — has the service been delivered or the product shipped? If not, cash received in advance is a liability (unearned revenue), recorded only on the Cash Ledger until the work is performed.

Failure mode 3: Expensing prepayments in full, or ignoring them entirely. Symptom: recording the whole $300 insurance payment as this month's expense, which wrongly turns the period into a $300 loss; or recording no expense at all, which overstates profit. Correction: split the payment by the amount of benefit consumed. Only the used-up portion ($50) is an expense; the rest ($250) is a prepaid asset. The Cash Ledger, meanwhile, records the full $300 out — the two ledgers intentionally disagree.

Failure mode 4: Collapsing the two ledgers into one "money score." Symptom: netting everything into a single number, so profit and cash are never separately visible, usually after mixing deposits, collections, and expenses in one list. Correction: physically build two columns for every case and assign each transaction to profit side, cash side, or both before computing any totals. The discipline of classification is what prevents the error.

Failure mode 5: Reading one ledger as a verdict on the business. Symptom: concluding "+ $350 cash means Lena is doing well" while profit is $0, or the reverse. Correction: always report both numbers and explain the gap — here, the deposit inflow and insurance timing explain it — before drawing any conclusion about performance or solvency.

## Guided Practice
All work happens in the app using the Lena's Landscaping data from the worked example, then a fresh transfer case. No spreadsheet or paper is needed; every number is provided.

Phase 1 — Setup and classification warm-up (6 minutes). Timer set to 6:00. Tool: the in-app two-column template with rows for the six Lena transactions. Behavior: for each transaction, choose one label — Revenue, Expense, Cash in, Cash out — and note that some need two labels. Expected output: a labeled list such as "T1: Revenue $800, no cash." Success criterion: all six transactions labeled with a money amount before the timer ends; if unsure, mark the transaction and move on.

Phase 2 — Timing decisions (8 minutes). Timer 8:00. Tool: the in-app decision prompts per transaction asking "was value earned this period?" and "did money move this period?" Behavior: for T3, compute the used-up insurance portion ($300 / 6 months) and justify the split; for T4, justify why the deposit is not revenue. Expected output: short written answers of one or two sentences per tricky transaction. Success criterion: each timing decision states the rule applied, not just the answer.

Phase 3 — Build the two-column summary (10 minutes). Timer 10:00. Tool: the in-app numeric entry fields for the Profit column and Cash column. Behavior: enter each amount in the correct column with its sign, then sum: accrual profit and net cash change. Expected output: a profit figure, a cash figure, and the three transaction types that caused the gap. Success criterion: both totals entered, and a written one-sentence explanation of why they differ. (Target: profit $0, net cash +$350 — verify against the answer key only after committing.)

Phase 4 — Self-check against the answer key (8 minutes). Timer 8:00. Tool: the in-app answer key with per-transaction feedback. Behavior: compare every classification and amount, not just the totals. For each mismatch, read the feedback and write one sentence identifying which rule was misapplied. Expected output: a mismatch list with rule corrections. Success criterion: every discrepancy diagnosed by rule name (earning test, matching, prepayment split), not by "I got it wrong."

Phase 5 — Revision pass (8 minutes). Timer 8:00. Tool: the in-app revision field. Behavior: re-enter any corrected classifications and recompute both totals; write a three-sentence summary of the Lena case in your own words — what was earned, what was collected, why the gap exists. Expected output: a corrected two-column summary plus the summary sentences. Success criterion: totals now match the key, and the explanation names the deposit and the insurance prepayment as gap drivers.

Phase 6 — Transfer case: Devon's Delivery Co. (6 minutes). Timer 6:00. Fresh data, same model. Devon's transactions this month: (a) delivers packages for a client, invoices $1,200, unpaid; (b) pays $700 cash driver wages for this month; (c) receives $400 cash advance from a customer for next month's deliveries; (d) pays $240 cash for a year's vehicle-insurance policy starting this month. Expected output: a two-column summary — accrual profit and net cash change — with each transaction classified first. Success criterion: classifications committed before computing; the insurance expense uses one-twelfth of $240 = $20. (Targets: profit $480; net cash −$540.)

Phase 7 — Final self-assessment (2 minutes, target within the practice budget). Review your Devon summary against the transfer answer key, log one rule you applied confidently and one you hesitated on, and set it as the focus for the Check Your Understanding questions.

## Check Your Understanding
Question 1: On June 5, Maya designs a logo and invoices the client $400, payable July 5. What does each ledger record in June? Checking rubric: Profit Ledger shows $400 revenue in June (earning test: work delivered); Cash Ledger shows nothing in June and a $400 cash-in event in July with no new revenue. Full credit requires stating that the July collection adds no revenue because it was already recorded.

Question 2: A client pays Dev a $600 deposit in March for an April installation. Dev's April profit is otherwise $1,100 of revenue and $900 of expenses. What is Dev's accrual profit in March, and what does the $600 deposit represent? Rubric: March accrual profit from this transaction is $0 — the deposit is unearned revenue, a liability, recorded on the Cash Ledger only. April profit is $1,100 − $900 = $200 plus, if the deposit work is completed in April, the $600 converts to April revenue, giving $800. Accept either reading if the timing assumption is stated explicitly.

Question 3: Using Lena's numbers, a classmate claims, "Lena's business improved because cash went up $350." What is wrong with the reasoning, and what two transactions best explain the cash–profit gap? Rubric: The conclusion confuses liquidity with performance. Accrual profit was $0, so nothing was earned overall. The gap is explained by the $500 unearned deposit (cash in, no revenue) and the $300 insurance prepayment (full cash out, only $50 expensed). Full credit requires naming both transactions and stating that both ledgers must be read together before judging business health.
