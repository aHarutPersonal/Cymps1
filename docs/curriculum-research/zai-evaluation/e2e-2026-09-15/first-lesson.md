# Track Cash and Profit Side by Side

## Why This Matters
John D. Rockefeller began his working life as an assistant bookkeeper, and the habit he built there, recording every figure with care and knowing exactly what money had actually arrived, carried him through the largest business career of his era. This lesson teaches that same foundational habit in its simplest useful form: the two-column ledger, where every business event is recorded once in a cash column and once in a profit column. Your own shop example shows why this matters. You described $100 of services sold on credit with $40 of expenses paid, and you suspected the cash answer of $60 profit was wrong. Your instinct was right: cash actually fell by $40 because the customer paid nothing, while profit was $60 because the work was earned. A business can show handsome profit on paper while its cash drains away, and more owners are ruined by that trap than by visible losses. Until you can hold these two numbers apart in your mind, every financial statement you read will blur into a single misleading figure. Once you can, the income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow statement become three clear windows onto the same room. This single distinction is the seam your whole twelve-week goal turns on, and it is learnable with small numbers and deliberate practice, exactly the method you said works for you. Roughly ninety minutes here, all inside the app with fictional data, builds the footing for every case that follows.

## Core Framework
The two-column ledger method rests on one discipline: every business event is examined twice, once for what it does to cash and once for what it does to profit, and the two answers are recorded separately even when they disagree. Before the mechanics, fix four definitions in plain language. Cash is money that has actually moved into or out of the business's hands, bank account or till, during the period. Profit, often called net income, is revenue earned minus expenses incurred during the same period, regardless of when money changes hands. Revenue is the value of work performed or goods delivered to customers. An expense is a cost consumed in earning that revenue, such as rent, utilities, or parts used up. Two further terms describe timing gaps. Accounts receivable is money customers owe you for work already delivered, a credit sale. Accounts payable is money you owe suppliers for goods or services already received but not yet paid. The cash column answers one question only: did money physically enter or leave, and how much. The profit column answers a different question: what did we earn and what did we consume doing it. A credit sale enters the profit column immediately and the cash column only when the customer pays. An unpaid utility bill enters the profit column as an expense immediately and the cash column only when you settle it. One category confuses nearly every beginner and deserves its own rule: long-lived equipment, such as tools, machines, or furniture expected to last more than a year, is an asset, not an expense. Buying a $300 tool set takes $300 out of cash on day one but takes nothing from profit that month, because the tool is not consumed; it is converted from one asset, cash, into another. Its cost affects profit gradually in later statements through depreciation, which you will meet in a later step and can ignore here. Money received is not automatically revenue, and money paid is not automatically expense; the test is earning and consuming, not receiving and paying. The two-column method also exposes the trade-off between the two accounting mindsets you will meet later by name. Cash accounting, which records only money movements, is simple and safe for tracking liquidity, but it wildly misstates performance in any month with credit sales or big purchases, showing a boom when cash floods in for old work and a collapse when equipment is bought. Accrual accounting, which records earnings and consumption when they happen, shows true performance but can hide a cash shortage, which is precisely how profitable businesses fail. The two-column ledger is a beginner's bridge between them: you keep both views side by side and reconcile them. The reconciliation is the payoff of the whole method. After totaling both columns, the difference between profit and the cash change must be fully explained by a short list of timing items: credit sales not yet collected, bills incurred but not yet paid, and equipment bought with cash but not yet expensed. If the numbers do not bridge exactly, an event has been classified wrongly, and the ledger itself tells you to go back and hunt. Two concepts are confused so often they deserve naming here. First, people confuse a credit sale with income the business can spend; the profit column celebrates it, the cash column is unmoved, and only collection converts it. Second, people confuse buying equipment with losing money; the cash column suffers, but the profit column does not, because the business still holds the value in another form. Hold those two apart and the three statements become readable. The income statement is essentially the profit column expanded, the cash-flow statement is essentially the cash column expanded, and the balance sheet, which you will study later, lists what the business owns and owes, including the receivables and equipment the two-column method taught you to place correctly.

## Worked Example
Practical scenario: Riverside Bike Repair, a one-person shop, June. Opening cash is $500. Eight events occur. Walk each through both columns, asking two questions every time: did money actually move, and was anything earned or consumed. June 2, cash repair jobs, $600 received. Cash column: +600. Profit column: revenue +600. The event hits both columns identically. June 5, shop rent paid in cash, $250. Cash: -250. Profit: expense -250. Rent is consumed in earning June revenue, so both columns agree. June 8, a repair job for a local school, invoiced at $400, payment due in July. Cash: 0, no money moved. Profit: revenue +400, because the work was completed and earned in June. This is the classic timing gap; the school's debt sits in accounts receivable. June 12, cash repair sales, $350. Cash: +350. Profit: revenue +350. June 15, purchase of a professional tool kit for $300 cash, expected to last several years. Cash: -300, real money left the account. Profit: 0. The kit is an asset, not a June expense; its value moved from cash to equipment, so profit is untouched. June 20, utility bill received for $120, payable in July. Cash: 0. Profit: expense -120, because June's utilities were consumed in June. June 25, cash repair sales, $280. Cash: +280. Profit: revenue +280. June 28, repair parts purchased for $180 cash and fully used in June jobs. Cash: -180. Profit: expense -180, because the parts were consumed earning June revenue; unlike the tool kit, they are used up. Now total each column. Cash column: +600, -250, +350, -300, +280, -180, which nets to a cash increase of $500, taking the balance from $500 to $1,000. Profit column: +600, -250, +400, +350, -120, +280, -180, which nets to profit of $1,080. Notice the two totals disagree by $580, and the disagreement is not an error; it is information. Reconcile it item by item. The credit sale added $400 to profit but nothing to cash: +400. The tool kit removed $300 from cash but nothing from profit: +300. The unpaid utility bill subtracted $120 from profit but nothing from cash: that reduces the gap by 120. Check: 400 plus 300 minus 120 equals 580, exactly the difference. The ledger bridges perfectly, so every event was classified correctly. Final explanations in one sentence each: Cash increased by $500 because the shop collected $1,230 in cash sales and paid out $730 for rent, tools, and parts, while receiving nothing yet from the school. Profit was $1,080 because the shop earned $1,630 of revenue, including the unpaid school job, and incurred $550 of expenses, including the unpaid utility bill, while the tool kit's $300 does not touch profit because it is an asset that still holds its value. Both statements are true simultaneously; they simply answer different questions.

## Failure Modes
Failure one: counting a credit sale as cash. Symptom: the cash column rises when an invoice is sent, or a learner reports cash up $60 on your original shop example. The ledger quietly disagrees with the bank account, and reconciliations never bridge. Correction: apply the physical test before recording anything in the cash column. Ask, did coins or a bank transfer actually occur on this date? If the answer is no, the cash column takes zero, whatever the profit column says, and the amount belongs in accounts receivable until collection. Failure two: expensing long-lived equipment immediately. Symptom: profit collapses by $300 in the month a tool kit, machine, or delivery bike is purchased, making a healthy month look like a disaster. Correction: apply the consumption test to the profit column. Ask, was this item used up within the year while earning revenue? If it will last, it is an asset purchase; record the full amount only in the cash column and leave profit alone. The expense will appear gradually later through depreciation. Failure three: forgetting unpaid bills as expenses. Symptom: a utility bill or supplier invoice due next month is left out of the profit column entirely, inflating profit, and the learner is surprised when profit falls after the bill is finally paid in cash. Correction: expenses belong to the period in which the cost was consumed, not the period in which it was paid. Record the $120 utility bill against June profit even though July cash will settle it. Failure four: mixing the opening balance into the cash change. Symptom: a learner reports ending cash of $1,000 as if the business generated all of it. Correction: the cash column measures change for the period only, $500 here; the opening $500 is a starting point, never an event. Whenever a reconciliation fails to bridge, return to these four tests in order, physical movement, consumption, timing of cost, and period change, and the misclassified event almost always surfaces within a minute.

## Guided Practice
Phase 1, terms check, 10 minutes, tool: in-app definition matching. Match each term, cash, profit, revenue, expense, accounts receivable, accounts payable, asset, to its plain-language definition from this lesson. Expected output: eight completed matches. Success criterion: all eight correct; re-read only the mismatched definitions before moving on. Phase 2, build the cash column, 15 minutes, tool: in-app two-column ledger template pre-loaded with the Riverside Bike Repair events and dates but empty cash cells. Work event by event applying the physical test: did money actually move on this date? Enter a signed amount or zero for each of the eight events. Expected output: eight cash entries plus a subtotal of the cash change. Success criterion: cash change of exactly +$500 with no hint use; if your figure differs, compare each event against the worked example before continuing. Phase 3, build the profit column, 15 minutes, tool: same ledger template, profit cells empty. Apply the consumption and earning tests: record revenue when work was delivered and expenses when costs were consumed, regardless of payment timing. Enter a signed amount or zero for each event. Expected output: eight profit entries plus a profit total. Success criterion: profit of exactly $1,080 computed independently, with the tool kit recorded as zero in profit and the unpaid utility bill recorded as an expense. Phase 4, reconcile and explain, 10 minutes, tool: in-app reconciliation box with three labeled slots, credit sale, equipment purchase, unpaid bill. Compute the gap between profit and cash change, then assign each timing item its signed contribution. Then type two one-sentence explanations using the sentence starters provided: one for why cash changed as it did, one for how profit was earned. Expected output: three reconciliation entries totaling $580 and two sentences. Success criterion: reconciliation bridges exactly, and each sentence names the correct figures and the correct reason. Phase 5, independent transfer case, 18 minutes, tool: in-app Lakeside Coffee Cart case, a different fictional business with fresh numbers: cash sales of $475, a $260 catering job invoiced for August payment, $150 rent paid in cash, a $90 coffee grinder bought in cash and expected to last years, $70 of coffee beans bought on account and fully used, and $55 of cash sales, with a $2,300 opening cash balance. First classify each event as cash-only, profit-only, both, or neither; then fill both columns; then state the cash change and profit figure. Expected output: seven classifications, two completed column totals, and the two final figures. Success criterion: cash change of +$555, profit of $610, and at least six of seven classifications correct on the first pass, without opening any hint. Phase 6, revision round, 8 minutes, tool: in-app feedback screen showing your Phase 5 answers against the key with specific explanations for any miss. For each incorrect classification, write one sentence naming the test you should have applied, physical movement or consumption, and the corrected entry. Expected output: one corrective sentence per error plus your revised totals. Success criterion: every error explained in your own words and the revised cash and profit figures now exactly match the key; if they do not, repeat Phase 6 once before ending the session.

## Check Your Understanding
Question one: On June 8, Riverside Bike Repair invoices the school $400 for completed work, payable in July. What goes in the cash column and what goes in the profit column for June? Checking rubric: cash column 0, profit column +400. Full credit requires naming the reason: money did not physically move, but the work was earned, so the amount is revenue recorded now and becomes cash only at collection. Question two: The shop buys a $300 tool kit in cash, expected to last five years. Which column changes, by how much, and why is this different from the $180 of parts used up in June? Checking rubric: cash column -300, profit column 0 for the tool kit, because a long-lived purchase is an asset whose value merely changes form; the parts are different because they were fully consumed earning June revenue, so they are a $180 expense in the profit column as well as a cash outflow. Question three: Lakeside Coffee Cart ends its month with cash up $555 but profit of $610. Name the timing items that create the $55 difference and give each item's signed contribution. Checking rubric: the $260 catering credit sale adds +260 to profit without cash, the $90 grinder purchase removes cash without touching profit, contributing -90, and the $70 of beans bought on account is an expense with no cash movement, contributing -70. Check: 260 minus 90 minus 70 equals 100, which does not equal 55, so if your items sum to something other than the actual gap, one event was misclassified; re-examine the grinder and the beans, since beans used and delivered are consumed, hence expensed, while the grinder is an asset. Score yourself: three correct with sound reasoning means you are ready for the next step; two correct means redo Phase 6; one or fewer means repeat Phases 2 through 4 before attempting a new case.
